# FocusLab evidence bank — the ONLY source of scientific claims

Tiers: STRONG = consistent findings across several RCTs or meta-analyses for the named outcome. MODERATE = several controlled studies or meta-analyses with small-to-moderate effects. MIXED = conflicting results, or effects depend on the person. EMERGING = few or small studies, or surveys. NOT SUPPORTED = well tested, benefit not found.
A claim applies ONLY to the outcome it names. Tiers are editorial judgments from a research pass in October 2026. References marked ✓ were checked online in that pass; the others are from memory, so open each one before publishing.

## breaks-energy — Short breaks between work blocks
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: vigor and fatigue.
Found: A 2022 meta-analysis of 22 studies found breaks of 10 minutes or less gave small boosts in vigor (d about 0.36) and small reductions in fatigue (d about 0.35).
Caveat: Mostly student and workplace studies; effects are small.
Refs: albulescu2022

## breaks-performance — Break ratios and task performance
Tier: MIXED. Outcome: task performance and concentration.
Found: The same meta-analysis found no clear overall gain in task performance (d about 0.16, not statistically clear); longer breaks were linked to larger performance gains. Fixed 25/5 versus self-paced breaks is unsettled: one 2023 student study (n=87) favoured fixed breaks for mood and concentration, while later student studies found Pomodoro raised fatigue faster or favoured self-paced breaks. One lab study found two brief task switches prevented the usual decline in a roughly hour-long vigilance task; follow-up work has not always replicated it.
Caveat: No work/break ratio is proven best. Keep timers flexible.
Refs: albulescu2022, biwer2023, ariga2011, behavsci2025, behavsci2026

## focused-attention — Focused-attention practice (breath counting)
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: sustained attention and executive attention (mostly from multi-week programs).
Found: A 2024 meta-analysis of 111 randomized trials (about 9,500 people) found mindfulness-based programs had small-to-moderate effects on sustained attention, executive attention and working-memory accuracy versus active controls (g about 0.19 to 0.39). A broad review of 44 meta-analyses found effects are typically smaller against active controls. Breath counting has been validated as a behavioral measure of mindfulness.
Caveat: The evidence is for multi-week programs; the effect of one 3 to 10 minute session is much less certain.
Refs: zainal2024, goldberg2022, levinson2014

## breathwork-mood — Cyclic sighing and box breathing
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: mood and physiological arousal (breathing rate). Not shown to improve attention directly.
Found: In a one-month randomized study, 5 minutes a day of breathing exercises improved mood and reduced anxiety; exhale-focused cyclic sighing improved mood and lowered breathing rate more than mindfulness meditation.
Caveat: Outcomes were mood and arousal, not attention. Remote study with self-reported mood. The pacing seconds in FocusLab are design defaults, not from the study.
Refs: balban2023

## nature-attention — A 40-second nature view
Tier: MIXED. Outcome: sustained attention.
Found: In one experiment (150 students), a 40-second view of a flowering green roof led to fewer omission errors and steadier responding than a view of a concrete roof. Systematic reviews find inconsistent attention effects of nature exposure overall, with clearer gains in working memory and cognitive flexibility and less reliable gains in attentional control.
Caveat: Lab tasks and picture or simulated views; small effects; real views were not tested. Mood benefits of nature are more consistent, but that is a different outcome.
Refs: lee2015, ohly2016, stevenson2018

## movement — A short bout of exercise
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: executive function (small, temporary effects).
Found: Meta-analyses find a single bout of exercise has a small positive effect on cognition, mostly executive function. High-intensity exercise had a small facilitating effect on executive function (d about 0.24 overall; about 0.34 versus rest), with no clear advantage over low-to-moderate intensity (d about 0.07).
Caveat: Bout lengths and tasks vary across studies; shorter bouts are less well studied; check that it suits your health.
Refs: moreau2019, chang2012

## phone-blocking — Blocking mobile internet
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: sustained attention, mental health and well-being.
Found: In a preregistered randomized trial (n=467), blocking mobile internet on phones for two weeks improved objectively measured sustained attention, mental health and well-being; participants also spent more time socializing, exercising and outdoors.
Caveat: The trial blocked mobile internet entirely, which is more than putting a phone out of sight. Compliance and participant selection may affect results; replication is needed.
Refs: castelo2025

## phone-presence — A phone merely nearby
Tier: MIXED. Outcome: attention and working memory.
Found: Earlier lab findings that merely having your own phone nearby reduces cognitive capacity have not always replicated.
Caveat: Treat "phone out of sight" as a low-cost habit to test, not a proven fix.
Refs: ward2017, castelo2025

## if-then — "If X happens, I will Y" plans
Tier: STRONG. Outcome: goal follow-through (not attention itself).
Found: A large meta-analysis of implementation-intention studies found that specific if-then plans help people start and carry out intended actions, with a medium-to-large effect.
Caveat: The evidence is for goal attainment, not for improving the ability to concentrate.
Refs: gollwitzer2006

## sleep — Sleep and alertness
Tier: STRONG. Outcome: attention lapses and alertness.
Found: Meta-analytic evidence shows short-term sleep loss reliably worsens attention and alertness, with some of the largest effects on simple vigilance tasks. The Psychomotor Vigilance Test was built to detect exactly this.
Caveat: Individual sensitivity to sleep loss varies widely.
Refs: lim2010, basner2011sleep

## noise — White and pink noise
Tier: MIXED. Outcome: attention-task performance.
Found: A meta-analysis of 13 studies found white or pink noise gave a small benefit on lab attention tasks for people with ADHD or elevated attention problems, and a small negative effect for people without (g about -0.21). No studies of brown noise were found.
Caveat: Lab tasks, mostly children and young adults. Test it on yourself.
Refs: nigg2024

## binaural — Binaural beats
Tier: MIXED. Outcome: memory and attention. Not built in the MVP.
Found: Meta-analyses report medium-sized average effects on cognition, anxiety and pain (g about 0.45 in 2019) and near-moderate effects on memory and attention (2023), yet individual studies conflict, especially for theta versus beta frequencies.
Caveat: Results depend on frequency, exposure time and timing; more robust trials are needed.
Refs: garciaargibay2019, binaural2023

## brain-training — Brain-training games
Tier: NOT SUPPORTED. Outcome: transfer to everyday attention or intelligence.
Found: A meta-analysis of 87 publications found working-memory training improves the trained tasks and similar ones, but no convincing improvement in broader abilities when compared with active control groups. A major review of brain-training programs reached similar conclusions.
Caveat: FocusLab uses tasks only to measure, never to claim training benefits.
Refs: melbylervag2016, simons2016

## body-doubling — Working alongside another person
Tier: EMERGING. Outcome: starting and sustaining tasks (self-reported).
Found: Many people report that working alongside someone helps them start and keep going, but direct controlled evidence is thin: mostly surveys, small studies and clinical consensus.
Caveat: Not an ADHD treatment. Out of scope for the MVP.
Refs: ara2025

## multitasking — Task switching and interruptions
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: cost of switching (stress, effort, slower next task).
Found: People who are interrupted often work faster to compensate but report more stress, frustration and effort. Thinking about an unfinished task can linger after switching ("attention residue") and slow performance on the next one.
Caveat: Mostly lab and workplace observation studies; effect sizes vary.
Refs: mark2008, leroy2009

## pvt-check — Brief reaction-time test as a measurement
Tier: MODERATE. Outcome: detecting sleep-loss-related lapses in alertness (lab version).
Found: A 3-minute version of the Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT-B) was designed to match the sensitivity of the standard 10-minute test to sleep loss. FocusLab's browser version is informal because screens, browsers and devices add timing noise.
Caveat: Compare only with yourself on the same device. Not a diagnostic tool.
Refs: basner2011pvtb
